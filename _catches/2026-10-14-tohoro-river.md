Same guide, different species. Today, we went for amemasu, which is both a species native to Hokkaido and also been the species that's been hardest for me to catch! The first time I caught an amemasu was in Lake Akan, although I have caught plenty of iwana elsewhere (amemasu is the sea run version of iwana).

Today, I tried lure fishing. It's a bit difficult to explain, but typically fly fishermen are against lure fishing. In general, I think lure fishermen have a worse rep in terms of respecting fish and there's less art in it. Fly fishermen are a bit elitist in this regard haha, but I feel the same way. In any case, lure fishing is extremely popular in Japan and a big part of fishing culture here! So I thought it'd be cool to learn it when the opportunity was presented to me.

Fishing today was in marsh rivers, as opposed to the stone rivers I'd previously been fishing in. I wasn't really sure what to expect, but I guess the most defining difference was that the riverbeds were made of sand/gravel in the marsh rivers, whereas the stone rivers were all stones (lol). Streams were tight and narrow, with fallen trees all over the river and heavy tree cover around. I presume that's also why lure fishing is necessary here, there's genuinely very little space to cast.

Amemasu are spawning around this time of year, so they're not really looking to eat anything. The strategy here, similar to the himemasu, is to get a lure or fly in front of their face and move it in a way to trigger an aggressive reaction bite. Since they're not really looking to eat, the bites are somewhat difficult to translate into a hook set, as they're more so hitting the "fish" with their mouth rather than trying to consume it. Lures here are also much more effective since it's easier to impart more action into it, and they're also much heavier so it is easier to get it to sink and stay at eye level with the amemasu. If the lure isn't right in front of their face, they typically won't bite.

The fish were everywhere, and a lot of the fish were tucked in little pockets around big fallen logs. I think the fallen trees causes the current to hit it and deflect around it, including downwards. This causes divots in the soft sand, creating this nice little cave almost for the fish to sit in. The water is also super clear and relatively slow however, so the fish spook very easily. Even casting a lure a little too close causes the fish to spook. That being said, I got really frustrated today with my guide, because he was doing the same thing again where he was just fishing by himself and getting really close to all the fish. Since the water was really clear, my suspicions were confirmed and I could see all the fish spook and run off in either direction. That was really frustrating, since by the time I got to the spots, the fish were all gone. I was still able to catch some fish however, but I noticed my best chances to catch fish were when he was taking a smoke break, or if I casted ahead of where he was going to walk/cast. I didn't want to be rude however and cast in front of him though, which is somewhat ironic since that's what he was doing repeatedly.
