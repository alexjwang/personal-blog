Today, my guide (Sapporo Streams) arrived and we went fishing on the Akan River. This was the first time I saw other fly fishermen fishing the same stretch of river, and not only that, it was basically four people lined up one run after another. As I got closer, I could see why: there were a ton of big rainbow trout visible in the water. I started getting a little dubious about how good the fishing was going to be consequently, as from my experience fishing other rivers in Hokkaido, big trout tend to be spooked the moment they see any human, let alone multiple humans casting heavy lures/lines at their face over and over again, day after day.

We waded a bit away from the other fishermen--apparently most Japanese fly fishermen rarely explore beyond the initial spot. Around one river bend, and there was basically nobody left to bother us. The river itself was beautiful--the sun wasn't the most cooperative, but the onsen water hitting the river mixed with the cold air meant that steam was coming off the surface of the river throughout the morning. Moments when sunlight peeked through the clouds and illuminated the fall leaves on the trees were fleeting but quite special.

It really didn't take long before I caught my first rainbow in a run, nymphing for the first time in what felt like ages (the Japanese guides in Tokachi are lowkey dry fly only fly fishermen). I think they're rather obsessed in that sense. It wasn't a crazy trout, probably in that 35cm range, but definitely a solid fish all things considered. I was pretty happy catching it!

Next, we went further up the river, where there was another deep pool in the middle of the river. I'm starting to learn where the highest probability spots where trout might be are. For nymphing, I like to figure out where faster currents either converge or by some mechanism (underwater rock, large divot in the ground) slow down. The faster currents act as a conveyor belt for food, and the trout hang out in the slow water because it doesn't actually take much energy to stay in place. Once identified, I like to cast just ahead of said pool, so that by the time the nymph sinks to the bottom, the current has just carried it into said pool. The advantage of these deeper faster pools with turbulent surfaces is that the trout also don't spook easily.

Anywho, at said spot, I hooked into a really nice 40cm+ rainbow. Now I started to see firsthand that the Akan Lake/River complex held some giant trout. I think catching a trout of this size would've been the biggest fish of the day on any other day. My feelings were confirmed yet again on the next stretch of river, where I caught another fish! Overall, wading was such a crazy experience. I would be walking through the river when I come face to face with a giant trout right at my feet at what felt like a distance at which I could just lunge and grab them. These fish were so desensitized to human presence, probably due to the combination of immense fishing pressure but also catch and release only regulations! Bait fishermen are also not allowed (thankfully) although lure fishermen are (boo!). It really made me think about how good fisheries could be if there were more spots that were catch and release only. At least this meant that people followed regulations, which is pretty expected for Japan!

We next moved up to another stretch of river, where there were several giant trout stacked in soft water next to the top of the run. Here's where I got to apply what I learned while fly fishing in Tokachi! We used a dry dropper rig here (on top was a grasshopper dry fly and at the bottom was a nymph). I cast right in front of one of the trout, and watched him come up and eat it! Another big one, and this one was extra satisfying to catch on my new fly rod/reel, plus also on dry fly! I'm sure they see fishermen throw dry flies at them all day, so getting a cast good enough to get them to eat was awesome.

In the afternoon, we went to the same stretch of river but walked the other direction. Around the bend there's not much fishable water, but then as you walk further you get to this nice long flat run that seemed great for dry fly. I got to showcase some of my improved fly casting skills here, honestly had a good time just walking and fishing.

As we were tying on a new fly, we heard a sound that almost sounded like an old man grunting while exercising? I looked into the distance and saw what looked like a shirtless man doing pullups? But then the white skin colored mass turned and stopped moving. It was absolutely still, so still that it made me doubt if it had moved in the first place. But after I told myself, nah that was definitely moving earlier, I started examining it more closely, when I saw four furry legs and a large brown mass. Asked my guide to come look at it, as if that was indeed what I thought it was, we could be in big trouble. My guide looked at it for 5 seconds and was like "yup that's a bear" and we walked away briskly. As we got further away, we saw another Japanese fly fisherman walking through the river, just where we/the bear was! We yelled at him, waited for him to (slowly) get to us just to be safe, and we all left after that. I was wondering when the first time I was going to see a brown bear in Hokkaido, kinda glad it finally happened (thankfully at a very safe distance)!
